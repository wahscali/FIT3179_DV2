Melbourne's network recovered its riders, not its rhythm

This page compares public transport use in Melbourne before COVID-19 (FY2018-19) with the latest full year (FY2024-25). Melbourne had the weakest recovery of the Australian capitals, with trips down 18%. The drop is uneven. Weekday peak travel into the city is still well down, while weekend and off-peak travel is close to 2019 levels.

1: Melbourne compared with the other capitals

Melbourne's patronage has recovered less than any other capital's. Within Victoria, the shortfall comes almost entirely from metro trains and trams.

Melbourne made 105.7 million fewer trips in FY2024-25 than in FY2018-19. That gap is about two thirds of Brisbane's yearly patronage.

Hobart and Darwin are close behind, but both are very small networks. Perth (+5%) is the only capital clearly above 2019.

Metro trains (−23%) and trams (−22%) are both about a fifth below 2019. Together that is roughly 100 million fewer trips a year.

Regional V/Line trains went the other way, up 19%. Metro buses are nearly back (−6%), likely because they carry more local trips.

2: Monthly patronage, 2018 to 2026

Patronage fell to almost nothing in the 2020 and 2021 lockdowns and has risen since. None of the three metro modes is back to its 2019 monthly average.

In August 2020 trains carried 9% of an average 2019 month. Trams carried 7%.

Over the 12 months to June 2026, trains averaged 83% of their 2019 level, trams 78% and buses 93%.

The dip every January is the summer holidays, not COVID.

3: Time of day and distance from the city

Not every kind of trip recovered at the same rate. Peak-hour commuting fell the most, while daytime and weekend travel held up. This fits with more people working from home on some weekdays.

The morning peak lost 30% of its entries and the evening peak 27%. Interpeak travel fell 17%, a little over half as much.

Switch to day of week and the gap is wider. Weekdays are down 27%, Saturdays only 9%.

Use the buttons under the chart to switch views. Hover a line to pick it out.

Each cell is compared with the whole network (−26%). Blue cells held up better than that, red cells fell further.

Stations 30 km or more out have off-peak travel back at or above 2019 (outlined). The biggest falls are early mornings 10 to 20 km out, down 39% and 43%.

Within 5 km the PM peak fell 34%. CBD stations only count entries, so these are mostly people heading home from work.

4: Where the recovery happened

The south-east growth corridor is the only area where train use is above 2019. Inner and eastern lines, where commuting by public transport was most common, are furthest behind.

Before COVID, more than a third of workers in Melbourne City (36%) and Stonnington West went to work by public transport. On the Mornington Peninsula it was 3%.

These inner areas had the most commuting to lose, which matches the line and station maps below.

The 2016 Census is used because the 2021 Census was held during a lockdown.

The Cranbourne line is up 35% and Pakenham is just above 2019 at +1%. Every other line is still down.

Lilydale (−36%), Alamein (−34%) and Hurstbridge (−32%) lost the most. Stony Point (−41%) is tiny and its counts are rounded.

Colour compares each station with the network change of −23%. Pale stations roughly match the network, blue did better and red did worse.

Of the larger stations, Cranbourne (+29%), Craigieburn (+22%) and Mernda (+18%) gained the most. All three are at the end of a growth-area line.

Merinda Park (+88%) and Officer (+70%) grew even faster, but are too small for the dot plot. They are labelled on the map instead.

5: The CBD stations

The five city stations still handle almost a third of all station entries. They lost more riders than the rest of the network, so their share is falling.

The CBD stations had 31% of all entries in FY2024-25, down from 34% in FY2018-19. They lost 22 million entries, more than any line.

The strongest gains are near the ends of growth-area lines, such as Mernda, Craigieburn, Cranbourne, Berwick and Officer.

Summary

Melbourne's network carries far more people than it did during the pandemic, but it has not returned to 2019 levels, and the travel that came back is different. Peak-hour trips into the city are down the most, while off-peak and weekend travel is close to normal. The only lines carrying more people than in 2019 serve the fast-growing south-east.

For planning, this suggests the weekday peak and the CBD matter less than they used to, and the outer south-east matters more.

Notes on the data

  • Station figures count people entering a station, not complete trips.
  • CBD stations record entries only, so morning arrivals there are not counted.
  • Stony Point line counts are very small and heavily rounded.
  • Some stations were rebuilt for level crossing removals between editions.
